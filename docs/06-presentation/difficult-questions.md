# Top 20 Tough Questions & Technically Honest Defenses

This document provides definitive, technically honest answers to the **20 most difficult, skeptical, and adversarial questions** that experienced professors, security auditors, or AI researchers will ask.

---

### Q1. "What is actually novel here?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
We do not claim to have invented multi-agent trust or vector databases. Our novelty is the **formalization and empirical evaluation of task-specific, evidence-based service selection compared against reputation-only scoring in autonomous agent ecosystems**. Specifically, we show how conditioning historical interaction traces on semantic task vectors breaks the "Halo Effect," how post-execution verification feedback prevents behavioral drift, and we quantify the trade-offs across five explicit operational metrics in a controlled simulation.

---

### Q2. "Isn't this just reputation?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
No. Reputation is an aggregate social metric summarizing what third parties claim about an agent globally. In our framework, reputation is merely one discounted prior input. Our actual decision variable is **task-specific empirical evidence**: verified execution logs of past tasks matching the exact semantic domain of the current request, weighted by verification rigor and recency.

---

### Q3. "Why do you need blockchain?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
We need blockchain strictly as a **tamper-evident append-only commitment log**. Without an immutable anchor, an agent could rewrite its local history to erase past failures. By anchoring Merkle roots of evidence batches to a smart contract, we achieve cryptographic non-repudiation. Blockchain does **not** verify AI outputs or decide trust; it simply ensures historical records cannot be rewritten post-hoc.

---

### Q4. "Why do you need RAG?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
As an agent executes thousands of tasks across diverse domains (code, math, SQL, translation), the evidence store grows large. RAG uses dense vector embeddings to retrieve only the historical records that match the semantic domain of the incoming task. This ensures an agent's trust evaluation is conditioned strictly on relevant experience rather than unrelated tasks.

---

### Q5. "Why do you need MCP?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
Model Context Protocol provides a standardized, vendor-neutral transport interface for tool invocation and schema declaration. Using MCP ensures our trust framework integrates seamlessly with modern frontier LLMs and heterogeneous tool ecosystems without requiring custom point-to-point wrappers.

---

### Q6. "Why not simply use a trust score?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
A single scalar score conflates competence, sample size confidence, and risk stakes. An agent with an 0.85 score could mean "high accuracy on 2 tasks" or "moderate accuracy on 5,000 tasks." Furthermore, a single score cannot represent domain-specific capability. Our framework separates Identity, Capability, Task History, Evidence Quality, Reputation, and Risk before making a gated decision.

---

### Q7. "How is this different from AgentReputation?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
AgentReputation (Chishti et al., 2026) designs decentralized consensus protocols to aggregate peer ratings into a global reputation score. Our project focuses on the client agent's **selection decision under risk**, demonstrating that global reputation scores are vulnerable to Sybil collusion and domain mismatch, and replacing them with task-specific verified interaction evidence.

---

### Q8. "How is this different from ERC-8004?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
ERC-8004 is an on-chain smart contract standard defining registry contracts for agent cards and feedback. Recent empirical research (Xiong et al., 2026) proved that ERC-8004 is severely compromised by Sybil reviews (up to 90.6%). Our framework sits inside the client agent, treating ERC-8004 feedback as an untrusted prior and adding semantic evidence retrieval and verification.

---

### Q9. "How do you know the evidence is trustworthy?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
We do not assume evidence is trustworthy by default. We enforce three safeguards:
1. **Direct Verification:** Evidence generated by our own client agent is validated inside a local deterministic sandbox.
2. **Cryptographic Provenance:** Records are signed by verified public keys and checked against anchored Merkle roots.
3. **Verification Rigor Weighting:** Deterministic proofs receive full weight ($q = 1.0$); heuristic reviews are discounted ($q = 0.5$).

---

### Q10. "Who verifies the verifier?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
This is the foundational epistemic boundary of all verification systems. If the verifier has a bug, it will produce erroneous evidence. We mitigate this by restricting high-confidence verification to deterministic domains (unit tests, mathematical solvers, schema validators) and executing them locally within the client agent's trusted boundary.

---

### Q11. "What if malicious agents create fake evidence?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
In our framework, third-party claims are not accepted as direct evidence. The client agent primarily trusts its own verified interaction history. If collaborative evidence sharing is enabled, evidence records must be accompanied by cryptographic signatures from authorized verifiers matching anchored state commitments.

---

### Q12. "How is task-specific trust different from reputation?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
Reputation is global, aggregate, and domain-blind (e.g., "Agent X has 4.8 stars"). Task-specific trust is local, empirical, and domain-conditioned (e.g., "Agent X has completed 14 SQL tasks with 100% unit-test pass rate, but has 0 history in Python execution").

---

### Q13. "Why 20 reliable, 5 unreliable, 5 malicious?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
It models a realistic open market:
- 66.7% reliable services representing the majority.
- 16.7% unreliable services representing infrastructural flakiness.
- 16.7% malicious services representing active adversaries and Sybil rings.
This allows us to evaluate whether our selection algorithm can successfully isolate both incompetence and malice.

---

### Q14. "Why these three strategies?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
- **Random Selection:** Scientific null baseline.
- **Reputation-Only Selection:** Current industry standard (ERC-8004 baseline).
- **Evidence-Based Selection:** Our proposed hypothesis.
Comparing these three is the minimum necessary to isolate whether task-specific evidence produces a statistically significant improvement over industry baselines.

---

### Q15. "How will you prove that the framework works?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
By running 10 paired simulation trials across independent random seeds and performing Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to demonstrate statistically significant reductions in Malicious-Agent Selection Rate ($MASR$) and increases in Task Success Rate ($TSR$) at $\alpha = 0.05$.

In accordance with academic research integrity, we do not publish fabricated or synthetic benchmark numbers. The simulation engine, agent population models, and metrics calculators are fully implemented in `src/`, and empirical figures will be published once physical multi-seed runs are completed.

### Q17. "What happens if two evidence sources conflict?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
The framework applies hierarchical resolution: it evaluates verification rigor ($q = 1.0$ deterministic vs. $q = 0.5$ heuristic) and recency. A recent deterministic failure heavily outweighs an older heuristic success.

---

### Q18. "What if the service behaves differently over time?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
Our trust engine applies exponential time-decay ($e^{-\lambda \Delta t}$) to all historical evidence. As an agent's performance changes, recent interactions dominate the score, enabling rapid detection of behavioral drift.

---

### Q19. "Why is x402 not in the main system?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
Because financial settlement should only occur after trust evaluation and result verification are proven reliable. Conflating autonomous micropayments with our initial trust prototype would introduce unnecessary regulatory and smart-contract complexity.

---

### Q20. "What happens when the trust system itself is attacked?"
**Honest Technical Answer:**  
If an adversary compromises the host machine running the client agent, the trust system is compromised (Assumption A1). However, against network-level attacks (Sybil reviews, prompt injection, payload poisoning), our multi-layered defense—Sybil discounting, sandboxing, and Merkle commitments—isolates the client from harm.
